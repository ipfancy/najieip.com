Omitted Claim, Maximum Fivefold Gone

November 24, 2026 · IP litigation · Punitive damages · Najie IP

The judge asked in court: 'Does the plaintiff wish to also claim punitive damages?'

You answered: 'Let's claim compensatory damages first.'

Just that one sentence. From May 1, 2026, the maximum fivefold damages are permanently foreclosed.

This is not alarmist. Fa Shi [2026] No. 7, officially titled 'Interpretation of the Supreme People's Court on Several Issues Concerning the Application of Punitive Damages in the Trial of Civil Disputes over Infringement of Intellectual Property Rights', was adopted at the 1972nd meeting of the Judicial Committee on April 7 this year, announced at a press conference on April 20, and came into effect on May 1. It consists of 14 articles. The title includes 'punitive', but the most piercing part is not the multiplier—it is the procedural time limits.

Three steps, the door narrows

First, where does the 'fivefold' come from? Article 12: The total amount of punitive damages shall not exceed five times the calculation base, and reasonable enforcement costs are calculated separately outside this total.

Now read Articles 2, 3, and 4 together—it is a path that gets narrower and narrower.

At the time of filing, Article 2 requires: To claim punitive damages, one must specify 'a clear amount of damages, the method of calculation, and the facts and reasons on which it is based'. A vague statement like 'request the application of punitive damages' is as good as not written.

Before the close of the first-instance hearing, Article 3: If a punitive damages claim is added at this time, the court shall permit it. This is the only lawful window to supplement the claim.

After the judgment takes effect, Article 4: If you claimed damages but did not claim punitive damages, and after the court's clarification you still do not claim it, then after the litigation ends, if you file a separate lawsuit based on the same infringement facts claiming punitive damages, it shall not be accepted.

There is also a segment in the second instance. The second half of Article 3: If a punitive damages claim is added only in the second instance, the court may mediate; if mediation fails, it shall not be supported. Under the old interpretation, there was still a path of 'informing to file a separate lawsuit' for omissions in the second instance; the new interpretation cuts that off.

If you miss it, it's gone.

In one sentence: In IP litigation, the first hurdle for punitive damages is 'whether and when it was claimed'. Whether it will be awarded comes later.

Already narrow, now welded shut

Some say: Even if claimed, it may not be awarded, so what's there to fear? Look at a set of numbers.

Since the original interpretation took effect in 2021, courts nationwide have concluded 1,471 IP cases applying punitive damages; in 2025 alone, there were 505 cases, with damages awarded of 1.8 billion yuan, accounting for one third.

Sounds impressive? Consider another figure. Zhu Li, Deputy Chief Judge of the Intellectual Property Court of the Supreme People's Court, conducted an empirical study in 'China Applied Jurisprudence', with a sample up to June 2025: In technology-related cases, 331 cases raised punitive damages claims, but only 35 were supported, a support rate of 10.6%; in the patent field, it was as low as 5.3%.

Even more striking are two accounts: 18.2% of parties did not claim in the first instance and waited until appeal; among cases not supported, 41.2% failed on 'unable to prove intent or serious circumstances', and 24.3% failed on 'unable to calculate the base'.

So the truth is: This door was originally only ten percent wide, and now it has been welded shut for those who did not knock in time.

The six words 'still did not claim after clarification' boil down to one sentence in court. Usually after evidence presentation and cross-examination, before court debate, the judge will ask in court: 'Does the plaintiff claim punitive damages?' The clerk records it verbatim in the transcript. If you answer 'no claim', or the agent pauses for two seconds and says 'as per the claims', that line goes into the transcript. After the judgment takes effect, that line is the basis for the court not accepting your separate lawsuit. I have seen many such cases: The right holder casually says in court 'not claiming for now', then tries to supplement in the second instance, but the judge only agrees to mediate, and if mediation fails, it is rejected; after the judgment takes effect, they still want to file a separate case to recover the fivefold, but receive the four words of Article 4: not accepted. Ultimately, the rules lock down the timing. The second you answer 'not for now', you are effectively signing a waiver; when the supplement window closes, there is no second notice.

How to respond? One core principle: Nail down the fact that 'it has been claimed' in the transcript and the complaint.

Before entering the door, print out the 'claims' page of the complaint separately and check whether the words 'punitive damages' appear at the end. If not, supplement now; it is still timely before the close of the first-instance hearing.

When the judge clarifies, do not answer vaguely 'not claiming for now'. If you really want to preserve it, state clearly in court 'reserve the right to claim punitive damages', have it recorded in the hearing transcript, and eliminate the constitutive element of 'still did not claim after clarification'.

In the complaint template, solidify the three-piece set: amount, calculation method, and facts and reasons. Missing any one, Article 2 is rendered ineffective.

Another landmine

Article 5: Claims for punitive damages against unfair competition acts other than trade secrets shall not be supported by the court, unless otherwise provided by law.

Translated into plain language: In anti-unfair competition cases such as counterfeiting and confusion, brand owners always think the Anti-Unfair Competition Law can 'double' damages, but it cannot. For the same infringement facts, the choice of cause of action directly determines whether doubling is possible. If it can be tied to trademark, patent, copyright, or trade secrets, do not rely solely on anti-unfair competition confusion.

One thing you can do today: Turn to the 'claims' section of any complaint not yet heard, and check whether the words 'punitive damages' appear at the end. If not, supplement now.
