Patent and Trademark Examination "15th Five-Year Plan" Released: 6 Changes Foreign-Related Applicants Should Watch

November 05, 2026 · IP Law · Aipunajie Patent Firm / Mili Law Firm

On November 3, 2026, the China National Intellectual Property Administration published the "Patent and Trademark Examination '15th Five-Year Plan' (dated October 19, 2026): four key tasks and 7 special projects, with the goal of basically building a world-class patent and trademark examination institution with Chinese characteristics by 2030.

For foreign enterprises applying for patents and registering trademarks in China, this document does not change any law, but it clearly sets out where examination resources will be directed over the next five years and what examiners will look at first. What will change is the scheduling timetable and the list of materials.

I. First, distinguish: the plan is not a new law

The plan is a work deployment of the CNIPA, not a law, and cannot be used as a basis for claims in individual cases. It does not even change the priority period: Article 29 of the Patent Law still provides 12 months for inventions and utility models and 6 months for designs; the grace period under Article 24 remains 6 months and recognizes only four circumstances.

What is written into the agenda is "studying and improving" — aligning with high-standard international economic and trade rules such as the CPTPP, and studying and improving systems such as patent priority, circumstances not destroying novelty, and deferred examination. There is no draft, no consultation draft, and no timetable; the target year is 2030. Therefore, scheduling should follow the current law, and the plan should be used only to observe direction.

II. Six points directly relevant to foreign applicants

The first point: priority and grace periods have entered legislative research. Article 18.38 of the CPTPP provides a 12-month grace period, while China's current period is 6 months and covers four circumstances: first disclosure for the public interest during a national state of emergency, first exhibition at an international exhibition hosted or recognized by the Chinese government, first publication at prescribed academic conferences, and disclosure by others without consent. Booth demonstrations, bidding documents, and customer trials are not included, and today they cannot be prepared on a 12-month basis.

The second point: on-demand examination is being rolled out comprehensively. There are four channels: fast, priority, deferred, and concentrated. The most practical for foreign applicants is deferred examination: where the market launch schedule is uncertain, one may proactively request postponement to avoid claims being fixed before the product is launched. On the other hand, the new Measures for the Administration of Patent Priority Examination (CNIPA Order No. 85), effective September 1, 2026, has already changed priority examination from "universal expediting" to "precise allocation."

The third point: examination cycles are only promised to be "leading," not promised in months. The plan's original wording is that "the invention patent examination cycle shall maintain a leading level among the world's major patent examination institutions under the same examination system." For market launch scheduling, financing valuation adjustment mechanisms, and licensing negotiations, do not write a specific cycle figure into contracts.

The fourth point: rules in emerging fields will change. Box 1 calls for research on: the definition of "person skilled in the art" and the scope of prior art in AI-assisted inventions, the inventor identity and patentability of AI-generated inventions, a declaration system for the use of artificial intelligence in patent applications, examination policies and pilots for standard essential patents, and submission rules for design objects and graphical user interfaces in digital fields such as VR/AR. For applicants in AI, SEPs, and smart terminals, the extent of AI participation and inventor attribution in the specification are changing from "no one asks" to "you will be asked."

The fifth point: the trademark side supports implementation of the new law. Carry out implementation of the revised Trademark Law and advance amendment of the implementing regulations; ex officio declare invalid registered trademarks that are deceptive, and ex officio revoke those used in a misleading manner; conduct penetrating supervision of malicious applications and list-based monitoring of agencies; explore new trademark types, goods classification in AI and VR fields, and an application intent commitment mechanism. For foreign-related brands selecting agents, compliance records will next matter, not just quotations.

The sixth point: international cooperation continues to expand. Consultations on WIPO framework topics, global governance in the field of artificial intelligence, the five offices of China, the United States, Europe, Japan, and South Korea and the BRICS mechanism, and continued expansion of the PPH cooperation network. For overseas firms, PPH remains a ready tool for shortening the cycle of Chinese cases, not a special channel requiring separate relationships.

III. Five things that can be done now

First, establish a priority chain ledger: first filing date, first filing country, subsequent countries and filing dates, remaining days, and whether copies have been submitted (16 months for inventions and utility models, 3 months for designs). The starting date is the day of the first filing date.

Second, establish a disclosure timing ledger: exhibitions, papers, bids, prototypes, trials, and launch events, recording the date and scope item by item. This table determines whether to file first or disclose first.

Third, complete R&D records for AI-related applications: whether AI participated, at which step it participated, and what substantive contributions people made.

Fourth, conduct a "use or not use" cleanup of the trademark portfolio: defensive registrations, registrations made to enter e-commerce platforms, and Chinese registrations never actually used carry rising risks; at the same time, archive evidence of use by trademark on an annual basis.

Fifth, treat on-demand examination as a scheduling tool: request priority examination where conditions are met, and assess deferred examination where the pace is uncertain. Examination pace should follow commercial pace, not the reverse.

IV. The value lies in direction, not today

What is most worth reading in this plan is not what it promises, but where money and people are being directed: rules for emerging fields, trademark examination quality, AI-assisted examination, and international cooperation. Build the ledgers well while the rules remain unchanged, so that when the rules change, there is no need to start over.
