The Second Gate: Outbound Transfer of Intellectual Property — Four Misjudgments and a Five-Question Self-Check

November 01, 2026 · Cross-border IP · Technology export · Aipunajie Patent Firm / Mili Law Firm

Let me start with a number that makes a lot of people doing cross-border work flinch: this measure was issued on 18 March 2018, and its name still carries the words "(Trial)". It has been on trial for eight years now.

Recently, while helping a client map out an overseas transaction, I ran into the same reaction more times than I can count. The client said: "We just want to grant an exclusive licence of our patent to that Hong Kong affiliate. We're not signing a transfer contract. Does this really fall under it?" I asked him to pull out Guo Ban Fa [2018] No. 19, the (Trial) Measures for the Administration of Outbound Transfers of Intellectual Property, and turn to the passage on "outbound transfer". There it is in black and white: a change of the rights holder, a change of actual controller, and an exclusive exploitation licence all count. In other words, even without a document called a "transfer contract", the system still treats this as an outbound transfer.

This is the second gate. The previous article covered the pre-application confidentiality review, which governs "will the technology leak". Today's gate governs "will the technology leave" at the moment the overseas deal closes. The filing window for both gates is before the contract is signed.

1. What the gate covers: four IP categories, three triggering acts

First, what does it cover?

This gate covers four categories of intellectual property: patent rights, layout-design rights of integrated circuits, computer software copyright, and new plant variety rights, including the rights to apply for these. Three actions count as an outbound transfer: a change of the rights holder, a change of actual controller, and an exclusive exploitation licence. The review has just two criteria: one looks at national security, the other at the capacity for innovation and development of core and key technologies in important fields. The second criterion does not ask how much your patent is worth; it asks whether the technology will stay in China. This is not a price review; it is a capability review.

There is one point I need to single out and correct, because so many people get it wrong: trademarks are not among the four categories. When companies hear "outbound transfers of intellectual property are subject to review", many assume that transferring a trademark to a Hong Kong company also requires filing. Wrong. A standalone trademark transfer follows the Trademark Law and does not touch this gate; only when it goes out together with technology might it be caught by that word "etc.".

2. Four misjudgments that cost deals

Let me go through the four misjudgments one by one.

Misjudgment one: thinking it only covers transfer contracts. As I just said, a change of actual controller and an exclusive licence are both in the definition. In a VIE restructuring, a red-chip redomiciliation, or an offshore fund coming in for control, the patent holder may not change in name, but the "actual controller" has changed, and it still has to pass the gate.

Misjudgment two: thinking trademarks also need review. I corrected that above: trademarks are not among the four categories.

Misjudgment three: thinking you can negotiate first and file later. This is the most critical one. The system is "licence-before-contract": without a Letter of Intent for Technology Export Licence, no entity or individual may conduct substantive negotiations abroad or make legally binding commitments; contracts for restricted export technologies only take effect from the date the Technology Export Licence is issued. If the order is reversed, the contract you signed itself does not take effect. The deal simply cannot be done, which is far more troublesome than a fine. At the registration stage it is even harder: where computer software copyright is not permitted to be transferred after review, the registration authority shall not process the change of ownership; patent transfer registration requires documents from the commerce department (Article 10 of the Patent Law). Not even a window for remedy is left.

Misjudgment four: thinking only prohibited exports are troublesome, while restricted exports are fine. It is exactly the opposite. Prohibited technologies are not allowed to be exported at all and cannot even reach this gate; this gate only opens for restricted technologies.

3. MOFCOM Announcement No. 48: a second procedure running in parallel

At this point, some may think this is an old 2018 document and far from their concerns. Don't turn the page just yet. On 16 October this year, MOFCOM and the Ministry of Science and Technology issued Announcement No. 48 of 2026, proposing to revise the Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited or Restricted from Export, covering 40 technologies in three fields: artificial intelligence, biopharmaceuticals, and photovoltaics — 23 in AI, 5 in biopharma, and 12 in solar; 14 proposed for prohibition and 26 for restriction, with comments open until 15 November. More important is a sentence easily buried by the headline: from the date of the announcement until the catalogue is formally revised and implemented, before exporting these technologies, registration must first be made on the Technology Trade Management Information Application on the MOFCOM unified business system platform. There are four situations requiring registration: transferring technology ownership abroad (such as an IP transfer), authorising domestic technology for use abroad, changing the owner or actual controller to an offshore entity, and other situations separately required by MOFCOM. These four situations are almost cast from the same mould as the three-part definition of "outbound transfer" in the No. 19 Measures. An outbound AI or biopharma deal now very likely steps on two procedures at once: registration under Announcement No. 48, which must be done right now; and, once the technology is listed as restricted, the review and licensing under the No. 19 Measures will also follow. We only saw this relationship by putting the two documents side by side ourselves; in the public articles we retrieved, we have not seen anyone piece these two layers together.

4. A five-question self-check to run before you sign

Finally, here is a self-check table with five questions to ask yourself before signing a contract:

  1. Is the counterparty an offshore entity (including Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan, and offshore affiliates)?
  2. Does the subject matter fall within the four categories — patents, layout-designs, software copyright, and new plant varieties (including rights to apply)?
  3. Is the action one of: change of the rights holder, change of actual controller, or exclusive exploitation licence?
  4. Is the technology in the restricted export catalogue, or among the 40 items in Announcement No. 48? If so, registration must be done right now.
  5. Are you on the technology export path or the national security review of foreign investment M&A path?

If you are unsure about even one of the five, hold the contract and clarify the characterisation before proceeding.

One point must be flagged clearly as our own analysis: the No. 19 Measures treat an exclusive licence as an "outbound transfer" requiring review, while Announcement No. 48 gives facilitation measures for biopharma "outbound licensing not involving a transfer of ownership". The two documents point in different directions on how to treat "licensing", so the statement "I am only licensing, so neither side needs to bother" cannot be used as a conclusion; you must assess separately based on the actual control rights and the type of licence in the transaction.

Going overseas is never a single checkpoint. The pre-application confidentiality review governs "will it leak"; the outbound transfer review at the time of the transaction governs "will it leave"; both gates are before the contract.
